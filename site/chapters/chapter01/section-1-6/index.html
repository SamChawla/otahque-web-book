Test Your Knowledge: Interview Questions

These five questions rehearse the chapter at interview level, one at each of five levels from junior to lead. L1 and L2 check what you have just learned. L3 asks how it works underneath. L4 and L5 ask about the design and leadership decisions that later chapters build on: attempt them now, and return to them when you reach those chapters. Use the readiness checkpoint to decide whether to continue. If an answer is difficult, revisit its worked example and explain the result in your own words. L4 and L5 are extension questions, not a gate to the next chapter.

L1 (Junior): What problem does Otahque solve, and for whom?

What the interviewer is testing: Can you state a product’s problem in terms of people and outcomes before naming any technology?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: Community organizers such as Sam run events with separate tools: an event page, a registration form, and a spreadsheet. Each holds its own version of who is coming, so someone must keep them in agreement by hand, and attendees such as Sai cannot be sure that their place is confirmed. Otahque keeps one current registration record. When an attendee registers or cancels, that record changes, the attendee sees a clear result, and the organizer sees the current list. The value is a reliable answer to “who is attending?”, not a website as such.

Behind the scenes:

the problem in one timeline (summary)

09:00  Sai submits the form          -> form tool: 1 row
09:05  Sam copies rows to the sheet  -> sheet: Sai listed
09:30  Raj cancels by email          -> sheet: not updated
10:00  Sam plans from the sheet      -> counts Raj by mistake

Evidence: Figures 1.1 and 1.2.

Common mistakes:

  • Starting with the technology (“it is a Django application”).
  • Listing features without the people who need them.
  • Claiming results, such as higher attendance, that nobody has measured.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “Who else is affected besides attendees and organizers?”
  • “How would you know that the product is solving the problem?”

L2 (Mid-Level): What is the difference between a role and a permission, and why does Otahque keep them separate?

What the interviewer is testing: Do you see that what a person may do depends on the situation, not only on who they are?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: A role is the part someone plays in a particular situation: visitor, attendee, or organizer. A permission says whether a person may perform a particular action. Roles depend on the event: Sam organizes the Python workshop but attends the photography walk, so he may update one event and not the other. If permissions were attached to the person (“Sam is an organizer”), he could edit an event he only attends. The question Otahque asks is always: may this person perform this action on this event?

Behind the scenes:

the same person, two events (summary)

Sam + Python workshop   -> organizer -> may update the event
Sam + Photography walk  -> attendee  -> may cancel his place
# the permission depends on the pair, not on Sam alone

Evidence: Figure 1.3.

Common mistakes:

  • Treating a role as a fixed property of an account.
  • Assuming organizers may do everything everywhere.
  • Treating a hidden button as an enforced permission.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “Where would you record which events a person organizes?”
  • “What should an attendee see when they try an organizer’s action?”

L3 (Senior): How do you separate business requirements from functional requirements, and why does it matter?

What the interviewer is testing: Can you keep the outcome an organization needs apart from the behaviour that supports it?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: A business requirement is an outcome the organization needs, such as organizers being able to rely on the current attendee list. A functional requirement is something the product must do to support it, such as letting an attendee cancel their own registration and updating the list. Keeping them apart lets you judge solutions: several behaviours may serve one outcome, and a feature that serves no outcome is a candidate for removal. It also gives evidence a place. Figure 1.5 links the outcome to the behaviour and to an example anyone can observe: Raj sees “Cancelled”, and Sam’s list no longer counts him.

Behind the scenes:

one outcome, its behaviour, and its evidence (summary)

business:    organizers can rely on the current attendee list
functional:  an attendee can cancel their own registration;
             the list updates
example:     Raj sees "Cancelled"; Sam's list no longer
             counts him

Evidence: Figure 1.5 and the business requirements in Step 2.

Common mistakes:

  • Writing a solution as a requirement (“send me a spreadsheet every evening”).
  • Stating outcomes nobody can check.
  • Stopping at business requirements, so that developers must guess the behaviour.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “Give a feature that would serve none of Otahque’s outcomes.”
  • “How would you check that a business requirement has been met?”

L4 (Staff): You are asked to plan the first version. How do you decide what is in scope and what waits?

What the interviewer is testing: Can you limit a release by outcomes and journeys, and record what you leave out?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: Start from the outcomes and the smallest set of journeys that achieves them: a visitor finds a published event, an attendee registers or cancels and sees a clear result, and an organizer sees the current list. Include what those journeys cannot work without, such as accounts, capacity, and a closing time. Defer what is valuable but not needed to reach the outcome, and write the deferred list down with reasons, so that it is a decision rather than an omission. Agree the list with the people affected, and say what evidence would bring an item forward.

Behind the scenes:

the first version, sorted (summary)

included:  published events, registering, cancelling,
           the current list, accounts, capacity, closing time
deferred:  payments, waiting lists, several communities
           (each with a reason and a revisit condition)

Evidence: the first-version scope in Step 1 and Figure 1.4.

Common mistakes:

  • Treating scope as a wish list.
  • Leaving deferred work unwritten.
  • Including something because it is easy to build rather than because a journey needs it.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “A sponsor wants payments in the first release. How do you respond?”
  • “What would make you bring a deferred item forward?”

L5 (Lead): Two stakeholders disagree about what the first version must do. How do you lead the team to a decision?

What the interviewer is testing: Can you turn a disagreement into a decision that is reasoned, owned, and recorded?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: Bring both positions back to the shared outcomes. Write each one as the need it serves and the outcome it supports, and check whether one behaviour can meet both. Often it can: an organizer’s need for a current list and an attendee’s need for a clear result are two views of the same record. When the positions truly conflict, name who decides, agree the criteria (who is affected, the risk, and the cost of waiting), and record the decision with the option not chosen and the point at which you will revisit it. Tell both stakeholders what was decided and why.

Behind the scenes:

a disagreement, written down (summary)

Sam:     "the first version needs a waiting list"
         need: fill every place  -> outcome: full events
Sponsor: "the first version must ship this month"
         need: start learning    -> outcome: a usable release
decision: ship without; record the waiting list as deferred,
          revisit if events regularly fill

Evidence: the deferred list and open questions in Steps 1 and 3; Chapter 5 introduces decision records.

Common mistakes:

  • Deciding by seniority or by whoever argues loudest.
  • Splitting the difference into a feature neither person needs.
  • Deciding without recording why, so the argument returns.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “How do you carry out a decision you disagreed with?”
  • “When is a disagreement worth a written decision record?”
