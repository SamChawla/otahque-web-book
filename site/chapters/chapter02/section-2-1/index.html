2.1 Needs and agreed outcomes

Sam has a list of workshop registrations. Raj has sent a cancellation message, but the list still includes him. Sai asks whether her registration succeeded. Before deciding what to build, we need to say whose difficulty we are addressing and what a useful result would look like.

Chapter 1 gave us a short brief. We will now turn it into a business requirements document, or BRD: a shared record of the problem, the people affected, the outcomes sought, and the boundaries of the work. Its purpose is to make our understanding available for review. Writing it does not make its contents agreed.

Who has a stake in the result?

A stakeholder is someone who is affected by the product or has a responsibility for it. A person can be a stakeholder without using every screen. The person responsible for community access, for example, has a stake in who may manage an event.

Sam needs a dependable registration list for planning. Sai needs to know whether her place is confirmed. Raj needs his cancellation to be reflected in the record. Soni needs clear event information before she decides to register. These names make our examples concrete; they do not represent four special accounts that the application must create.

Chapter 1 described three roles: visitor, attendee, and organizer. From this chapter on, we need two more words. A member is a person with an Otahque account. An attendee is a member who holds a registration for a particular event. Sai is a member whenever she signs in, and an attendee of the workshop while her registration is active. Soni remains a visitor until she creates an account.

The community administrator is a new stakeholder. The administrator manages accounts and access, including who may organize events. We still need to decide how organizers are appointed. Naming a responsibility helps us identify a question; it does not answer every permission question.

Separate a need from a proposed solution

Suppose Sam says, “Send me a spreadsheet every evening.” That is a proposed solution. Ask what decision the spreadsheet supports. Sam might need to prepare enough seats using the current registrations. An evening export could already be out of date when Raj cancels the next morning.

The useful outcome is that Sam can plan from the current registration record. The product behaviour that supports it is access to an up-to-date list for the event he manages. The format of an export can be decided later. This distinction keeps the reason for a feature visible when its design changes.

Show answerHide answer

Answer. No. It names a possible delivery method. We must first establish what Sam needs to decide and how current the information must be. Here, the desired outcome is reliable preparation using current registrations.

Trust, privacy, and accountability

Trust here has a concrete meaning: Sai’s displayed registration status and Sam’s list must agree about whether she has a place. If Raj cancels successfully, both views must reflect his cancellation. Conflicting answers can lead to unnecessary travel, mistaken preparation, and extra messages to resolve the uncertainty.

Privacy means setting boundaries around personal information. Soni needs public event details to decide whether to attend; that need does not justify showing her everyone else’s registration information. Attendees manage their own participation, and organizers manage their assigned events.

Accountability means being able to establish who was responsible for an important action. If access to an event is changed, the community needs a way to investigate who made that change. We are recording the need here. Later chapters will decide which records support it, who may read them, and how long they are kept.

What do we actually know?

Our workshop is a fictional teaching example. It makes the problem understandable, but it is not evidence that real organizers experience the same difficulty or would adopt Otahque. Evidence is information we have actually gathered, such as an observed workflow or an interview record, with its source and context.

A hypothesis is an idea we intend to investigate. “Small volunteer-led communities would spend less time reconciling registrations if the records stayed connected” is a hypothesis. Independent technical communities and college clubs are proposed audiences to investigate. Neither demand nor time savings has been established.

A useful investigation would ask an organizer to walk through a recent cancellation: where it was received, which records changed, who updated them, and what remained uncertain. Record what happened separately from our interpretation. Do not turn one person’s account into a claim about all communities.

To investigate time savings, first measure the current work for a defined task, such as reconciling registrations before an event. That starting measurement is a baseline. Later, compare the same task under comparable conditions. We have no such measurement yet, so our draft must not promise a percentage reduction.

Constraints and open questions

A constraint is a boundary our solution must respect. For the book’s first release, we have chosen one community, free events, and individual registration through member accounts. These are project decisions, not findings from customer research. They keep the first product small enough to build and verify.

Closing times and re-registration after cancellation remain open questions. Record them instead of quietly choosing answers while writing code. Later volunteer assignments and sponsor reporting are possible extensions; they do not grant additional access or expand this first release.

Que. 3 What could those three sentences say?

Show answerHide answer

Answer. Outcome: Raj can tell that his cancellation succeeded. Boundary: Raj can cancel only his own registration. Open question: until what time may he cancel? The first explains the benefit, the second limits authority, and the third identifies a decision still needed.
