Test Your Knowledge: Interview Questions

These five questions rehearse the chapter at interview level, one at each of five levels from junior to lead. L1 and L2 check what you have just learned. L3 asks how it works underneath. L4 and L5 ask about the design and leadership decisions that later chapters build on: attempt them now, and return to them when you reach those chapters. Use the readiness checkpoint to decide whether to continue. If an answer is difficult, revisit its worked example and explain the result in your own words. L4 and L5 are extension questions, not a gate to the next chapter.

L1 (Junior): What makes a good user story, and how is it different from acceptance criteria?

What the interviewer is testing: Do you know what a story is for, and what makes a behaviour checkable?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: A story states who needs a capability, what it is, and why it matters: “As a member, I want to cancel my own registration, so that my decision is reflected in the event record.” It starts a conversation; it is not a specification. Acceptance criteria are observable conditions the behaviour must satisfy before we accept it, often written as Given/When/Then: given Raj has an active registration and the cancellation deadline has not passed, when he cancels, then his registration is cancelled, the active count falls by one, and he sees the result. The story says why; the criteria say when the work is done.

Behind the scenes:

one story, three criteria (summary)

Story:  As a member, I want to cancel my own registration,
        so that my decision is reflected in the event record.
AC-1:   before the deadline, cancelling releases one place
AC-2:   after the deadline, cancelling works; marked late
AC-3:   cancelling someone else's registration is refused

Evidence: the registration and cancellation criteria in Step 2.

Common mistakes:

  • Leaving out the reason (“so that ...”).
  • Writing criteria nobody can observe (“cancelling is easy”).
  • Describing only the path where everything goes well.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “Write the criterion for cancelling after the deadline.”
  • “Who should write acceptance criteria, and who should agree them?”

L2 (Mid-Level): What is an edge case, and how would you find them for event registration?

What the interviewer is testing: Can you search a rule’s boundaries systematically instead of waiting for bugs?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: An edge case is a situation near a boundary, where behaviour is easy to get wrong. Walk each rule to its limits. Capacity: the last place, and two requests for it. Time: the closing instant itself; in Figure 2.6 a request at 17:59:59 is accepted and one at 18:00 is refused. State: cancelling twice, or registering while already registered. Failure: a response lost on the way back (Figure 2.8). Write each one as a Given/When/Then example, so the expected result is decided before anyone writes code.

Behind the scenes:

edges of the registration rule (summary)

time:      opening instant, 17:59:59, 18:00
capacity:  19 of 20, 20 of 20, two requests for one place
state:     already registered, cancelled, cancel twice
failure:   request lost, reply lost

Evidence: Figures 2.6 to 2.8 and the rules in Step 3.

Common mistakes:

  • Checking only typical values.
  • Leaving the closing instant ambiguous (“until 18:00”).
  • Assuming one person acts at a time.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “Is 18:00 itself included, and where is that written down?”
  • “Which edge case worries you most, and why?”

L3 (Senior): Explain what an invariant is, and why concurrent requests threaten it.

What the interviewer is testing: Do you understand that a rule checked at one moment can be broken by overlapping actions?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: An invariant is a condition that must always remain true. For Otahque, active registrations never exceed capacity. Suppose two members request the last place at the same moment (Figure 2.7). Each request reads “19 of 20 places taken”, each decides there is room, and each records a registration: 21 of 20. Checking before saving is not enough when the processing overlaps. The rule must be enforced where the result is recorded, so that exactly one request succeeds and the other is told the event is full. Later chapters do this in the database; the requirement’s job is to state the invariant and the expected result for both people.

Behind the scenes:

the race, step by step (summary)

A reads count = 19      B reads count = 19
A: 19 < 20, so register  B: 19 < 20, so register
A writes registration    B writes registration
count = 21               # the invariant is broken

Evidence: Figure 2.7 and the capacity invariant in Step 3.

Common mistakes:

  • “We check capacity before saving, so it is fine.”
  • Trusting the count the browser displayed.
  • Specifying only what the successful member sees.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “How would you test two requests that overlap?”
  • “What exactly should the second member see?”

L4 (Staff): How do functional and quality requirements differ, and how would you write a quality requirement someone can check?

What the interviewer is testing: Can you turn words such as “fast” and “accessible” into something that can pass or fail?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: A functional requirement says what the product does: an attendee can cancel. A quality requirement says how well, or under what conditions: the journey works with a keyboard alone, on a narrow screen, or within a stated time. A checkable quality requirement names the situation, the measure, and the threshold. For example: “Every step of registering for an event can be reached and completed using only a keyboard.” Without a measure, nobody can accept or reject the work. Quality requirements often shape the design more than features do, so they belong in the requirements document from the start.

Behind the scenes:

from a word to a requirement (summary)

word:        "accessible"
situation:   registering for an event
measure:     can each step be reached with the keyboard alone
threshold:   every step, with focus visible
check:       a person completes it without a mouse

Evidence: the quality expectations in Step 4 and the PRD in Table 2.2.

Common mistakes:

  • Adjectives without measures.
  • Treating quality as polish to add at the end.
  • Setting a threshold with no way to measure it.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “Which quality requirement would change Otahque’s design the most?”
  • “How would you measure it before the first release?”

L5 (Lead): How do you keep requirements, checks, and evidence traceable as a product grows, without burying the team in documents?

What the interviewer is testing: Can you make traceability useful and proportionate, and keep evidence honest?

Flip to check your answerFold answer back

Perfect answer: Give each requirement a stable identifier, such as BR-1 or PR-2, and link it both ways: back to the need it serves, and forward to the controls, checks, and evidence (Figure 2.9). Keep the links where people already work: identifiers in story titles, test names, and change descriptions, with one requirements document as the source. Record evidence only after a check has actually run, with its date and source; a planned test is not evidence. Review the links at each milestone: a requirement with no check is unverified, and a check with no requirement may be unnecessary. Trace product rules and risks closely, not every wording change.

Behind the scenes:

one register row, before and after a check (summary)

PR-2  need: Raj can tell his result   control: server rule
      check: cancel twice, inspect the count
      evidence: (empty until the check runs)
      -> later: "passed, test run on <date>, <where>"

Evidence: the working register in Table 2.3.

Common mistakes:

  • Copying requirements into several documents that drift apart.
  • Marking a requirement done because code exists.
  • Keeping a large matrix that nobody updates.

Follow-up the interviewer might ask:

  • “A test passes, but the requirement it traces to has changed. What do you do?”
  • “What is the least traceability you would keep on a small team?”
