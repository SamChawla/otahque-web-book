Why This Matters

Since Chapter 19, every event in Otahque has been typed by you, in the shell or, since Chapter 23, in the admin site. Neither is a place for Sam. The shell is for developers, and Chapter 23 decided that the admin site is the administrator’s tool: it reads every typed time in UTC, it lets an account with one permission change every event, and it has no idea that Sam organizes only his own. Sam needs a page of Otahque’s own where he can describe an event, see each mistake explained beside the field it belongs to, and save it without anyone else seeing it before it is ready.

Sam lives in India, and his next event is an online Django clinic for a group in the United States, at 19:00 New York time on 3 December. A page that reads his 19:00 in UTC stores a time five hours early; one that reads it in his own zone stores it ten and a half hours early. The time means what the event’s zone says it means, so the form must read it there, and it must say so on the page. And whatever the form allows, it must never let a typed value set something Sam should not control, such as whether the event is published.

That is what forms are for. A form is the part of a web page where people type values and send them to the server, and Django’s form library is the part of Django that checks those values, explains what is wrong, and hands your code clean, typed values. This chapter builds Otahque’s first one: Sam’s New event page, which saves a draft. Visitors cannot propose events; a visitor still only reads published ones.

By the end of the chapter, you will be able to:

  • explain how a browser sends a form, and what Django does between the request and your view’s first line;
  • write a Form and a ModelForm, and say why a ModelForm’s fields list is an allowlist;
  • decide where each rule lives: a field, the form’s clean(), the model, or the database;
  • read typed times in the event’s own time zone, and refuse a time that a clock change skips or repeats;
  • protect a page with sign-in, a permission, and an ownership check, and show a draft only to the people who may see it;
  • render fields with labels, help text, and errors that browsers and screen readers connect, and prove the protection against forged requests with a test.

Editing a draft and the form’s validation in depth are Chapter 25, publishing Chapter 26, and members’ own sign-in pages Chapter 27. Showing each viewer times in their own zone is Chapter 28, and the design of roles and permissions Chapter 30.
