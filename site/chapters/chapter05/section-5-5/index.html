5.5 Recording the first architecture decision

In a year, someone will ask why Otahque renders its pages on the server. The volunteers who compared the options may have moved on, and the discussion will be half-remembered. A short written record keeps the reasoning available to whoever asks.

Starting point: the comparison in Section 5.4. Your output is a draft architecture decision record, a practice record of your own, and the chapter’s glossary and readiness check.

What an architecture decision record is

An architecture decision record, or ADR, is a short document that captures one significant decision, the situation that led to it, and its consequences. Michael Nygard proposed the form in 2011 so that teams would stop losing the reasons behind their designs. A record is usually a page or two, numbered in sequence, and kept with the project’s code so that it changes alongside the system it describes.

A useful ADR states a title that names the decision; its status; the context, meaning the requirements, constraints, and pressures at the time; the options considered; the decision; its consequences, both helpful and costly; and the changes that would justify revisiting it. It does not repeat the whole comparison; it summarizes it and points to where the detail lives.

A decision has a status

The status tells a reader whether to rely on the record. A proposed record is written for review and not yet agreed. An accepted record has been agreed by the people responsible for the project. A rejected record was considered and not adopted; keeping it saves repeating the same discussion. A superseded record has been replaced by a later one that links back to it. Once a record is accepted, it is not rewritten to match a later choice. A new record supersedes it, so the history of the design stays visible.

Que. 15 The team later adds an API so that a partner’s app can list Otahque events. Should they edit the accepted record for the first release so that it seems to have included an API all along?

Show answerHide answer

Answer. No. Write a new record for the API decision and link it to the earlier one. If the new decision replaces the old one, mark the old record superseded. If it adds to it, as the planned API in Chapters 47 to 54 would, both records remain valid. Editing the old record would erase the reasoning a future reader needs.

Our first decision record

Table 5.4 is our draft. As the project’s first decision record, it is numbered ADR-001; later records continue the sequence. Its status is proposed, because no one responsible for a real Otahque project has reviewed it, and this book does not invent an approval. The following chapters build on this proposal as the working design; if review changes it, a new record will say so.

Notice what the record does not decide: which database runs in production, how the application is hosted, or how pages are styled. Each is a separate decision with its own context, recorded when its chapter needs it. One decision per record keeps each record short, reviewable, and easy to supersede.

Once the project exists, keep decision records as numbered Markdown files in a folder such as docs/adr/ inside the repository, so that they are versioned with the code. Chapter 8 introduces Git for that purpose. For now, your paper copy is enough.

In practice: making a disagreement reviewable

Situation. Two contributors disagree about rebuilding the event list with client-side rendering.

Application. Ask the proposer to write a short decision record: context, options, and consequences. The disagreement becomes specific: which requirement does the change serve, and what does it cost?

Consideration. Writing the record does not settle the question. Someone responsible for the project still has to accept or reject it, and the status should say which.

Try it yourself

On paper, write a second, smaller record: should Otahque show event dates and times in the event’s own timezone or in each visitor’s device timezone? Use the parts of Table 5.4. Base the context on Chapter 2’s expectation that times are clear wherever a person acts on them, give at least two options, and choose an honest status.

Que. 16 What would a useful practice record contain?

Show answerHide answer

Answer. Title: show event times in the event’s stated timezone. Status: proposed, because nobody responsible has accepted it. Context: Chapter 2 requires a date and timezone wherever a person acts on a time; members may view from devices set to other timezones; a deadline must refer to one instant. Options: the event’s timezone, always labelled; the visitor’s device timezone, converted; or both together. Decision: show the event’s timezone, labelled, everywhere, and consider adding the visitor’s local time later. Consequences: organizers and attendees share one unambiguous time; a remote attendee must convert it until a local equivalent is added. Revisit if: online events with attendees in many timezones become common. Another decision is acceptable if its context and consequences support it.
